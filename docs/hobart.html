Hobart, the mountain and the evening sun

Hobart sits at the eastern foot of kunanyi / Mount Wellington, 1271 m high and only seven to twelve kilometres from most of the city. For much of the year the evening sun goes down behind the mountain long before it would set on a flat horizon. Over the next eight weeks, as the summer solstice approaches, the sun sets a little further south each evening, and that makes two things easy to check from your own street.

Never look straight at the sun, even when it is low and dimmed, and not through binoculars or a camera viewfinder. Watch the light on the ground, the shadow edge, or your phone's screen.

1. The evening the sun goes behind the summit

From any spot east of the mountain, the summit sits at a fixed direction and height in the sky. The sun's evening path crosses that exact point on just one or two evenings either side of the summer solstice: once in late spring, as sunsets move south, and once in late summer, as they move back north. On those evenings you can watch the sun's disk slide down behind the summit itself.

This prediction needs no elevation model at all, only the summit's position and height, where you stand, and the sun. So it tests the sun position, the timekeeping, refraction and the earth's curvature separately from the terrain data.

The coloured band shows where each evening of the next eight weeks lands, assuming you stand at sea level. The band moves north across the city: around the waterfront and Bellerive at the end of October, Rosny in early November, Montagu Bay late in November, North Hobart and Rose Bay in early December. North of the grey edge (New Town, Moonah, Lindisfarne, Geilston Bay) the summit lies further south than the sun ever sets, so it never happens. Those suburb dates are rough. 2. When the sun leaves your street

The second check uses the terrain. For a chosen evening, the map shows the last minute of direct sun on the ground across the city, with the shadow of kunanyi and its foothills moving east as the sun goes down. Areas close under the mountain, such as Fern Tree, South Hobart and Lenah Valley, lose the sun hours before the eastern shore. The page reads the Tasmania 2 m DEM directly from its cloud-optimised file, at 16 m for the city and 64 m for 40 km around it. That is roughly 10 to 20 MB to download, and some seconds of computing.

3. Last sun over the coming weeks

For a single spot, the page traces the skyline in every direction (40 km out, with curvature). Then for each evening of the next eight weeks it finds the last minute the sun is above that skyline. How to check, in practice

  • Where you stand, exactly. Use the location button, or drop a pin in a map app, and note what you are standing on (beach, footpath, deck) and roughly how high your eyes are above the ground.
  • The time, to the minute. Phone clocks are set from the network and are good to well under a second. A photo or short video of the moment, taken on a phone, records the time for you.
  • What moment. For the summit, note when the sun is centred on it, or when its edge first touches and last leaves it. For the last sun, note when the last sliver disappears. That is the upper edge of the disk, a minute or so after the centre.
  • Which evening. Pick a clear one. Cap cloud on the summit is common, and haze or smoke near the horizon can make the last minutes hard to call. The BOM forecast for Hobart helps, and so does looking out the window at five o'clock.
  • Expect agreement to within about a minute and a day for the summit, if the position is right. For last sun, expect a few minutes in open ground; more where a tree, house or the near side of a hill makes the skyline rather than the mountain.

What the data and the method can and cannot say

The data

  • Bare earth. The Tasmania 2 m DEM is ground level only. In a street the real skyline is often a roof, a tree or a power pole, and these are not in the model. A prediction that says "sun until 19:40" means the land does not block it until then.
  • Mixed sources. About 65% of the state's DEM comes from LiDAR; the rest is interpolated from contours and spot heights at an effective 10 m. Around kunanyi and Hobart much of the ground has been flown with LiDAR, but not every hill on the skyline of every suburb.
  • Its age. The file is dated 2021. Earthworks, quarries and cuttings since then are not in it.
  • The summit point. Its position and height come from public gazetteer values. If they were ten metres out sideways, that would be under a tenth of a degree from eight kilometres away.

The method

  • Coarse cells round off ridges. The evening map uses 16 m cells for the city, and the far skyline uses 64 m. Averaging into cells lowers sharp ridge tops a little, so the model's skyline sits slightly low and its last-sun times run slightly late, most of all for spots close under a ridge. The skyline probe for a single spot reads the same cells, so it has the same bias.
  • Steps in time. The map samples the sun every 5 minutes, so its times are good to about two and a half minutes. The chart steps by the minute against a skyline traced every degree of azimuth.
  • Refraction. The air bends low sunlight upward, raising the sun by 0.1 to 0.2 degree when it is 5 to 10 degrees up, and more nearer the horizon. Standard values are used for the sun and for the line of sight to the summit (k = 0.13). Cold air pooled over the Derwent on a still evening can change that a little. At these heights that is seconds to a minute, more for the last sun on the eastern shore, where the mountain's skyline is lowest.
  • Earth curvature. Included: at 10 km the ground falls away by about 7 m, enough to lower the summit by 0.04 degrees.
  • The sun's disk. The sun is half a degree across and moves its own width in about two minutes here. Predictions are for its centre, so the first touch and the last sliver come about a minute either side.
  • Your position and height. Over seven to twelve kilometres, the summit alignment moves by one to two days for every 100 m you stand further north or south. Height matters much less: 10 m higher moves it by a tenth of a day or so. The card works out both for your spot. Near the grey edge, where the sun only just reaches the summit's direction around the solstice, the dates spread out and small moves matter more.

Put together, the summit alignment is a sharp test of the geometry, sun and clocks, almost free of the terrain data. Last sun is a test of the terrain data and the skyline method, but blurred by whatever stands between you and the mountain. That blurring is the most useful thing to learn about: where a well-placed observation disagrees, the DEM, the cell size or the bare-earth assumption is the likely reason.
